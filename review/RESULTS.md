# Current-protocol proof results

This report covers the current protocol’s claims, supporting proofs, and existence checks. Alice is the caller and Bob is the person being called. For delegated calls, a number’s owner (for example, a business) authorizes another device to call on its behalf.

**69 current-protocol checks**: **69 verified**. These totals include supporting checks and existence checks; they are not a count of separate security guarantees.

**Timeouts in the full verification suite: 0 total** — **0** among the protocol checks below and **0** among the [supporting checks in MODELING_FINDINGS.md](../MODELING_FINDINGS.md#supporting-verification-checks). These counts concern declared lemmas. Separate derivability-audit warnings/time limits are documented in [ABSTRACTIONS.md](../ABSTRACTIONS.md#derivability-audit) and are not included here. The shared manifest includes all checks.

## How to read the results

- **Proved in model:** the statement holds in every execution covered by the model, with the assumptions in the statement and the scope below.
- **Proved: execution exists:** Tamarin proved that at least one execution satisfies all the conditions in the statement. The existence check passes. This establishes that the described behavior is possible in the model; it does not mean that it occurs in every execution.
- **Counterexample found:** an allowed execution violates the stated current-protocol claim; that claim is not proved.
- **Unresolved: timeout:** the search ran out of time. Neither a proof nor a counterexample has been established by that run. Other unresolved labels identify missing runs, unfinished supporting checks, or conflicting results.

For successful protocol flows, this is an **executability proof**. For example, proving that Alice and Bob can complete authentication establishes that the model permits the stated request, answer, and identity-display steps. The attacker can discard messages, so we cannot promise that every call completes. The successful execution shows that completion is possible when the required messages are delivered. It also checks that the model has not accidentally made the intended flow impossible.

Some existence checks instead establish that a particular disclosure or compromise scenario is possible. In every case, **Proved: execution exists** proves the statement in that row. Security guarantees that must hold across all allowed executions are checked separately and labeled **Proved in model**.

A device’s **long-term keys** are its private keys for authenticating or decrypting messages. A **shared key** is the separate secret Alice and Bob use to encrypt a particular exchange. Exposing one is not the same as exposing the other. Where a statement says keys are never exposed, that means throughout the entire modeled execution, including after the call. Statements about later exposure say so explicitly.

Claim titles below link to the corresponding formal statement. The appendix retains the original lemma IDs, exact verdicts, proof steps, and timings.

## Scope of these results

These are checks of separate protocol components under idealized cryptography. The network attacker can intercept, change, replay, and create messages, but can only break the modeled cryptography through the capabilities explicitly provided. Enrollment is trusted. Credential generation and validation are assumed external operations; Julep’s checks concern disclosure control and transport confidentiality. Identity guessing, real expiry/revocation behavior, and implementation bugs are outside these checks. Authentication and credential exchange start with an agreed pairing key; the complete sequence from real pairing through all later exchanges has not been proved as one combined protocol. See [ABSTRACTIONS.md](../ABSTRACTIONS.md) for the precise assumptions and limits.

## Which paper requirement does each result address?

The S1–S6 names below come from the paper’s Security & Privacy Requirements. Number authorization and additional credential requests are separate research objectives in the paper, so they retain their objective numbers. Each result’s requirement column names its target and explains whether it checks part of the requirement, supports another proof, checks whether a described execution is possible, or tests a known limitation. A link to a requirement does not mean that the result proves the entire requirement.

The C-PAIR, C-AUTH, C-DEL, and C-ODA identifiers are claim labels from the modeling analysis, not the paper’s requirement numbers. The statements and scope in this report define the exact formulations checked for the current protocol. For S5, authentication key agreement and ODA replacement-key secrecy are separate proved properties; the latter permits theft of other shared keys. Credential ordering, consent, and presentation transport confidentiality support S1 under the stated credential-interface assumptions, while attribute values are public inputs.

## Pairing: preparing Alice and Bob to authenticate calls

Alice is preparing to call Bob. Pairing associates their phone numbers with device keys and establishes a shared secret for later call authentication. Alice saves an unconfirmed key before Bob finishes pairing. Saving it alone does not establish that Bob sent a response: the protocol permits a holder of Bob’s public enrollment token to construct a response Alice accepts. Her state is confirmed only when Bob’s subsequent call-authentication response verifies.

For a delegated call, an owner such as a business authorizes Alice to call using its number. Alice uses her own device keys. Permission to place those calls does not also authorize her to answer calls to the business number.

The recipient-side secrecy and agreement statements below apply when the caller device has an honestly issued enrollment record, including an enrolled delegate. They do not promise secrecy against a caller whose keys the attacker owns.

## Call authentication: knowing who called and who answered

Alice calls Bob. Bob checks the incoming request before displaying the caller’s identity. If Bob chooses to answer, he sends an authenticated response; Alice then checks it and displays Bob’s identity. Answering is an explicit action, not merely receiving a ring or displaying a name.

For a delegated call, Bob sees the owner’s identity, while the delegate’s device authenticates the request. The checks distinguish the owner of the displayed number from the device placing the call.

This model starts with Alice and Bob already holding the same shared key. It checks one authentication round, including the replacement key they store afterward. The shared key that encrypts the exchange is separate from each device’s long-term keys. Several checks allow the former to leak while requiring the latter to stay private.

## On-demand credentials: choosing whether to share evidence during a call

After call authentication, either person can ask the other for credential evidence—for example, evidence of an affiliation. The person asked can consent to share it or decline. ODA/ICV are the protocol’s names for this exchange. Here, Alice remains the caller and Bob the person who answered, regardless of who asks for evidence.

A presentation is the payload produced by an external credential component for a particular request. Credential generation and validation are outside Julep’s verification scope; the model assumes their behavior. Julep controls when this payload is sent and protects its transport to the peer. Alice can receive it normally; the confidentiality question concerns the network attacker.

The transport claim concerns presentation material supplied privately by that component, with no separate publication or disclosure by it. Attribute values are public inputs in the current model, so this is not a proof that facts such as someone’s employer are secret. Transport cannot make an already public payload secret.

The model assumes an agreed pairing key and includes a shortened call-authentication exchange before credential requests. Disclosure control (authentication, answer, and consent) and transport confidentiality are separate guarantees. The combined recipient statement follows from the answer rule and transport confidentiality; it adds no independent requirement.

Each device retains one current shared key for the exchange and replaces it on an update. For example, call authentication produces K1. The first credential round encrypts its request and presentation under K1, derives K2 from K1, its authenticated transcript, and a fresh DH secret, then replaces K1 with K2 and deletes K1. If an attacker steals K1 while it is current, the attacker can read round 1 and remember K1 after deletion. Knowing K1 alone does not reveal K2: the update also requires the fresh authenticated DH secret. The next key and relevant long-term private keys must remain unexposed.

The key-secrecy proof covers the keys produced by the call-authentication prefix and every subsequent credential update. It permits the attacker to remember shared keys stolen during other rounds. The transport proof then establishes that the presentation has no route to the network attacker while its protecting key and the relevant long-term private keys remain unexposed. These are conditional guarantees with temporary DH secrets unavailable to the attacker.

Shared-key theft is limited to the key a device currently retains for that exchange. Once both devices replace and delete a key, it can no longer be stolen from their state; any copy the attacker already obtained remains known. The compromise event exposes the shared key alone, not temporary DH secrets. See ABSTRACTIONS.md for the exact scope.
